Introduction to Nucleic Acids

All living organisms contain both DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) and RNA (ribonucleic acid). This chapter focuses on their location, structure and function. It also explores the discovery of DNA, its role in the human body and how it replicates through protein synthesis, a vital process for life that involves DNA and RNA working together.

Revision of Cellular Structure

Before going further with DNA and RNA, it is important to know the location and functions of certain organelles, illustrated in Figure 1 below.

Figure 1: Structure of a cell

The cytoplasm is a watery substance in which the organelles of the cell are suspended. It is the site where most metabolic reactions take place.

Ribosomes are small organelles found either free in the cytoplasm or attached to the endoplasmic reticulum. They are the site of protein synthesis.

The nucleus is the control centre of the cell and contains most of the cell's DNA.

Figure 2: Parts of the nucleus

The nucleus consists of the following main parts:

  • The double nuclear membrane, which encloses the nucleus and contains pores that allow substances to move in and out
  • The nucleoplasm, a jelly-like fluid found within the nucleus
  • The nucleolus, a dark structure within the nucleoplasm that produces ribosomes
  • The chromatin network, which contains DNA that carries genetic information.

The Structure of Nucleic Acids

Within the nucleus, the chromatin network contains DNA, which carries the genetic information of the cell. This information determines the characteristics and functioning of an organism, and RNA plays a role in how it is used within the cell. Both DNA and RNA are essential for life and belong to a group of organic compounds known as nucleic acids.

Figure 3: A nucleotide

Both DNA and RNA are built from repeating monomers called nucleotides linked together in long chains. Each nucleotide consists of three basic components:

  • a phosphate group
  • a sugar molecule
  • a nitrogenous base

In DNA, the sugar present is deoxyribose, while in RNA the sugar is ribose. The type of nitrogenous base present, as we will cover, also helps differentiate DNA from RNA.
